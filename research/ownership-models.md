# Ownership models — prior art

Reference notes on organizations that structurally protect a mission
from ownership pressure. This is a **research note** — part of the
governance process as groundwork: solutions may cite it as evidence
(see [03-amendment-process.md](../03-amendment-process.md), Step 2),
but it binds nothing on its own. If anything in this file disagrees
with the charter, the charter governs.

The question each entry is examined against: **who holds the residual
claim, and who holds control?** A mission clause is only as strong as
the ownership mechanics behind it.

## Proton (Proton AG / Proton Foundation)

Privacy company (Mail, VPN, Pass, Drive), Geneva, founded 2014 at
CERN. Revenue is subscriptions — no ads, no donations.

**Structure (since June 2024):** the for-profit Proton AG is
controlled by the Proton Foundation, a Swiss non-profit foundation
endowed by the founders with a donation of their shares. A Swiss
foundation has no shareholders, cannot be bought or sold, and its
board is legally bound to the foundation's purpose, under state
supervision. As the largest voting shareholder, the Foundation can
block any change of control — Proton cannot be acquired against the
mission.

**What it is:** a takeover-proofing structure (the
"steward-ownership" pattern — Bosch, Zeiss, Purpose Foundation
variants). It answers "can this be bought and gutted?" with no.

**What it is not:** a redistribution structure. Observed gaps:

- The Foundation pledge is 1% of *net* revenues, "when conditions
  allow" — discretionary, not a bylaw obligation. The rest of the
  revenue runs through Proton AG like any company.
- Stock options are explicitly preserved for hiring. Equity upside
  exists; only the acquisition exit is removed.
- No ownership percentages are disclosed. "Primary shareholder" and
  "largest voting shareholder" are the only published terms —
  compatible with anything from 51% to a blocking minority.
- "Employees own the vast majority of shares not held by the
  foundation" is a statement about the share register, not the
  headcount: most non-Foundation shares sit with employees, but
  nothing is claimed about how many employees hold shares, or how
  concentrated the block is. Standard startup vesting suggests it is
  front-loaded by hire date and seniority.
- Founders (Andy Yen, Dingchao Lu) sit on the Foundation's board of
  trustees — supervision is legally real but not arm's-length.

**Contrast with a workers' cooperative:** steward-ownership fixes the
mission by mission clause plus foundation control; the surplus and
membership questions stay answered the startup way. A cooperative
answers them by attaching the residual claim and the vote to the work
itself, not to hire date or negotiation.

## To research

- Robert Bosch GmbH / Robert Bosch Stiftung — the oldest large
  example; ~94% of shares held by the charitable foundation, voting
  rights separated into an industrial trust.
- Carl Zeiss AG / Carl-Zeiss-Stiftung — foundation-owned since 1889;
  the original statute (Ernst Abbe) included worker protections.
- Purpose Foundation — modern steward-ownership toolkit (golden
  share, veto structures) used by Ecosia, Sharetribe and others.
- Mozilla Foundation / Mozilla Corporation — non-profit owns
  for-profit; instructive failure modes around executive pay.
- Signal Foundation — non-profit operating an app directly, funded
  by donation/endowment rather than subscriptions.
- Godot Engine / Godot Foundation — non-profit foundation (2022,
  Netherlands) employing maintainers; funded by donations and
  corporate members; no for-profit arm at all.
- Bevy Engine / Bevy Foundation — US 501(c)(3) (2024) around a
  volunteer-driven engine; instructive for how small a foundation
  can be and still hold the trademark and pay a lead.
- Linux — plural structures worth separating: the Linux Foundation
  (a 501(c)(6) trade association funded by corporations — not a
  charity, and instructive for that); kernel copyright held by
  thousands of individual contributors (no entity can relicense);
  distributions with their own models — Debian/SPI (charity),
  openSUSE (corporate-sponsored), Arch (informal).
